# One Model, Two Outcomes: Why AI Amplifies People Differently

Short answer. AI does not give everyone the same lift. In controlled studies it often helps less experienced people most on routine writing and support tasks, yet it also makes weak work look polished and can reduce the effort people put into checking it. What separates good outcomes is not the model. It is four habits: setting a clear aim, giving real context, judging the output against criteria, and learning from each round. This article explains the evidence, offers a framework for building those habits, and shows how a team can make them normal.

In many offices there are now two colleagues with the same AI seat, the same training video and the same weekly meeting where someone says "use it more". Three months later one of them has a small library of prompts, a list of what the tool gets wrong and a habit of rejecting the first draft. The other still types a one-line request, accepts what comes back and sometimes forwards it with the formatting intact.

Nothing about the software differs. What differs is how each person works with it, and that is something a business can see, measure and improve. This is the practical question behind the debate on whether AI makes people smarter or lazier: under which conditions does it do which?

The idea that results depend on the person at the keyboard is not new. Its most useful form comes from combining several lines of research that rarely get read together. This article does that, adds a simple framework and finishes with what a manager can do on Monday.

## What the research actually shows

The evidence does not support a single slogan. It supports several findings that pull in different directions.

| Study | Setting | What it found | What it does not show |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Noy and Zhang, preregistered experiment | 453 college-educated professionals on occupation-specific writing tasks | ChatGPT users took about 40 percent less time and output quality rose about 18 percent. Differences in performance between workers narrowed | Results for other kinds of work or for long-term use |
| Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, NBER working paper | 5,179 customer support agents with an AI assistant | Productivity rose about 14 percent on average and about 34 percent for novice and low-skilled workers. Experienced workers saw minimal impact | That the effect holds outside support work |
| Doshi and Hauser, Science Advances | 300 writers of short stories, 600 raters | The most AI access raised novelty by 8.1 percent and usefulness by 9 percent. Less creative writers gained most. Stories became more similar to each other | That the result applies to professional writing at scale |
| Dell'Acqua and colleagues, Organization Science | 758 consultants using GPT-4 | Large gains inside the tool's strong range. Outside it, AI users were less often correct, and their recommendations still read as coherent | That current models fail in the same places |
| Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon researchers | Survey of 319 knowledge workers | Higher confidence in AI was associated with less self-reported critical thinking effort. Higher self-confidence was associated with more | That AI use causes less thinking. This is a correlation in self-reported data, and six of seven authors work at Microsoft |
| MIT Media Lab researchers, preprint | 54 participants writing essays, 18 in the last session | Participants who used an AI assistant showed lower measured neural connectivity during essay writing and reported less ownership of their text | That AI lowers intelligence or harms the brain in the long run. The sample is small, the work is a preprint, and the authors' interpretation is not established |

Sources are listed at the end. Two cautions apply to the whole table. Many of these studies used earlier models, and none measures a whole business over years.

## So does AI widen the gap or close it?

Both, depending on what the gap is about.

For producing a competent first draft, a standard reply or a short piece of writing, the studies above suggest AI narrows differences in output. A newer employee with a good assistant can reach a level that previously took longer. Brynjolfsson and colleagues offer suggestive evidence that the tool spreads the practices of stronger workers.

For deciding what is worth doing, noticing that an answer is wrong and choosing among many options, the picture is different. The Dell'Acqua study found that polished output outside the model's reliable range made errors harder to catch. The Microsoft survey found that people confident in the tool checked less. Doshi and Hauser found that individual stories improved while the group's stories converged. These findings suggest that execution is levelling up while direction and judgment are becoming the differentiating part.

This two-gap reading is a hypothesis built from separate studies, not a measured result. It is useful for planning because it tells you where to invest: not in more prompts for everyone, but in the judgment around them.

## The Kubera Direction Loop

This is a Kubera AI planning heuristic, not a validated method. It names four habits that tend to separate people who get useful results from those who get filler. They run as a loop, not a list.

| Habit | The question it answers | A weak version | A strong version |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aim | What exactly do I want, and for whom? | Write a description of this product | Write a description for a procurement manager comparing three suppliers, 80 words, factual, no superlatives |
| Brief | What does the model need to know that I know? | No context | Pasted price list, two past replies in our tone, a list of words we avoid |
| Judge | How will I decide if this is good? | It looks fine | Checked every number against the price list, compared tone with our two examples, cut anything I cannot support |
| Learn | What do I change for next time? | Start again from scratch | Saved the corrected version and the rule it taught me in a shared note |

Aim and Brief are the part most people have heard of. Judge and Learn are where results diverge, because they are the habits that keep working after the model gets better, and they are the ones that research suggests erode when people trust the tool too much.

### Aim: decide before you ask

Vague requests produce average answers because the average is all the model has to go on. Spend one minute writing down the reader, the purpose, the length and one thing the result must not do.

### Brief: hand over what is in your head

A model cannot know that your best client dislikes exclamation marks or that your delivery terms changed last month. Context you supply is the largest single lever you control, and it is a skill, not a talent.

### Judge: use criteria, not a feeling

When the model produces five options, the person who wins is the one who can say why option three is better than option two. Write the criteria before you look: accuracy, tone, fit with the customer's question, legal risk. A short checklist beats a general impression.

### Learn: keep what the round taught you

Each round teaches something: a word to ban, a missing fact to include, a type of question where the tool guesses. If it stays in your head it disappears. If it goes into a shared document it becomes the team's advantage.

## Practice mode and autopilot mode

The same tool can train thinking or replace it. The difference lies in the order of work.

**Autopilot mode.** You hand over the task, receive the result and send it on. Speed is high and learning is low.

**Practice mode.** You think first, even briefly, then use AI to widen, challenge and sharpen. The model becomes a sparring partner.

Four small rules turn a session into practice mode.

- **Draft first, if only three lines.** Write your own rough answer, then ask the model to critique it. You keep ownership, and you see the gap between your thinking and its.
- **Ask for objections, not answers.** "What would a sceptical finance director challenge in this proposal?" works better than "improve this proposal".
- **Run the explain-back test.** Before you send anything, can you explain every claim without looking at the screen? If not, you do not yet own the text.
- **Ask for the evidence.** Request the line in your source that supports each claim. Anything without a line is a guess.

The MIT preprint measured activity during one writing task in a small group. It does not show lasting effects on intelligence or memory, and the Microsoft survey shows an association, not a cause. The reasoning here is simpler and does not depend on either: effort spent checking is the part of the work that builds judgment, so keep some of it.

## When AI makes a thousand options, taste becomes the job

Generating variants is now cheap. Ten ad headlines, thirty product photo concepts, five versions of a customer email: all take minutes. The scarce work moves to selection, and selection needs standards.

Without standards, two things happen. People pick whatever looks most polished, and the whole company's output starts to look the same, which matches the convergence Doshi and Hauser measured in stories.

A business can protect itself with a small, practical asset: a good-looks-like library. It contains five to ten examples of work the company is proud of, five examples it rejected and one line each on why. New people and models are briefed with it. Brand voice, product photography rules and tone for difficult customers all fit this format.

| Write review criteria per task | Judgment becomes teachable | One short checklist for each recurring task |

## Illustrative scenario

Illustrative scenario, not a specific Kubera client. A 30-person distribution company gives two sales coordinators, Anna and Marek, the same AI assistant. Both must answer supplier questions and prepare quote comparisons.

Marek pastes three quotes and types: "compare these". The table comes back tidy and he sends it to the buyer. One delivery cost was missing from a quote and the model filled it with a plausible number. The buyer finds it two days later.

Anna starts differently. She writes the aim: a table for the buyer, who cares about total landed cost and delivery time. She briefs the model with the company's payment terms and asks it to write "not stated" wherever a quote is silent. She judges by checking every cell against its PDF, then asks the model what a sceptical buyer would question. When she finds an error she adds a line to the team's shared note: ask for "not stated" on every missing field. Marek reads the note a month later and starts doing the same.

The model was identical. The outcome differed because of four habits, and two of them are now written down for everyone. That last step, turning one person's lesson into a team rule, is where an individual advantage becomes a business one.

## A self-check in seven questions

Answer yes or no. Each no is a place to improve.

- Do I write down who the reader is and what the result must do before I ask?
- Do I paste in my own context rather than expecting the model to guess?
- Do I know three things this tool gets wrong in my work?
- Do I check facts against a source and not against my feeling?
- Do I sometimes draft first and ask the tool to critique?
- Can I explain each claim in what I send without the screen?
- Does anything I learned last week exist in a shared note?

## Frequently asked questions

### Does AI make people less intelligent?

The evidence does not say so. A Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon survey found that confidence in AI went with less critical thinking effort, and a small MIT preprint measured lower neural connectivity during an essay task for participants using an assistant. The first is a correlation in self-reported data, the second is an unreviewed preprint on a small sample, and neither shows lasting harm. They show that effort placed on checking and thinking is a choice.

### Does AI help beginners or experts more?

On routine writing and support tasks, studies found beginners gained more. On judgment-heavy tasks outside the model's strong range, the picture changes, and experts who can spot errors are better placed.

### Why does the same model give me worse results than a colleague?

Usually because of the aim, the context and the review, not because of a secret setting. Compare your request with theirs.

### Is prompt engineering the skill that matters?

Writing a clear brief matters. Clever phrasing matters less than context, criteria and the habit of checking.

### What is the biggest risk of using AI without checking?

Polished errors. The consulting study found that AI users' answers outside the model's range were less often correct while still reading as coherent.

### How do I stop all our content sounding the same?

Brief with your own examples, keep a library of approved and rejected work, and have a person choose among variants against written criteria.

### Should juniors use AI?

Yes, with the order of work protected. Let them draft first, then compare with the model and explain the differences.

### How do I measure whether the team uses AI well?

Track rework time, errors caught before sending and customer outcomes. Logins and prompt counts say little.

### Do we need rules for AI use?

A short set helps: what data may be pasted, which outputs need a source line and who approves customer-facing text. For data questions see [AI data safety for European businesses](/blog/ai-data-safety-european-businesses). This is not legal advice.

### Will better models remove this problem?

Better models may reduce some errors, but deciding what to do and judging whether the result is good remain human work for now. The habits above are designed to stay useful as models improve.
